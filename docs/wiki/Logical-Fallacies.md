# Logical Fallacies

Fallacies are taught as failures of reasoning, not vocabulary terms to memorize.

## Red herring

An irrelevant issue distracts from the proposition.

**Diagnostic question:** Does this statement make the proposition more or less likely, or merely change the subject?

## Begging the question

A premise assumes what the argument is supposed to establish.

**Pattern:** X is true because X is true in different words.

## Straw man

A weaker version of the opponent's claim is substituted for the real claim.

## Equivocation

A key word changes meaning during the argument.

This is one reason definitions are agreed upon first.

## False dichotomy

Only two possibilities are presented when additional alternatives exist.

## Ad hominem

The person is attacked instead of the proposition or evidence.

## Appeal to ignorance

A claim is treated as true because it has not been disproved, or false because it has not been proved.

## Circular reasoning

The conclusion ultimately depends upon itself as support.

## Hasty generalization

A broad conclusion is drawn from insufficient observations.

## Post hoc reasoning

Because one event occurred after another, the first is assumed to have caused the second.

## Fallacy laboratory

Students should encounter fallacies inside realistic arguments and identify:

1. the exact sentence containing the problem;
2. the fallacy;
3. why it is defective;
4. how the argument could be repaired.

The highest-level exercise asks students to repair rather than merely label a bad argument.
