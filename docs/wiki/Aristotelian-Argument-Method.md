# Aristotelian Argument Method

The course uses a simplified classical structure suitable for student practice.

## Required lesson structure

### Question
A precise issue capable of competing answers.

### Proposition
The claim being defended.

### Proof 1
Evidence or reasoning supporting the proposition.

### Simplified syllogism
**Major premise:** a general rule.  
**Minor premise:** a relevant case.  
**Conclusion:** what follows if both premises are accepted.

The same structure is repeated for Proof 2 and Proof 3.

### Counter-proposition
A serious alternative position.

### Restatement
The original proposition expressed again after the evidence and counterargument have been examined.

## Example

**Question:** Would limited rendering be a rational design strategy for a large simulation?

**Proposition:** A sufficiently large simulation could reduce computational cost by generating some representational detail only when needed.

**Proof:** Computing unnecessary detail consumes resources.

**Syllogism**

Major: Systems that avoid unnecessary computation can use fewer computational resources.  
Minor: Limited rendering avoids some unnecessary computation.  
Conclusion: Limited rendering can reduce computational resource use.

Notice what the conclusion does **not** say. It does not establish that our universe is simulated.

## Validity versus truth

A syllogism can have a valid form while containing a false or unsupported premise. Students therefore inspect both:

1. Does the conclusion follow?
2. Are the premises justified?

That distinction prevents “it sounds logical” from becoming a substitute for evidence.
