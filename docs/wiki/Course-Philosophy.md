# Course Philosophy

## Reasoning before conclusion

The course is designed around disciplined inquiry. Students should be able to understand a position they disagree with, identify the strongest evidence offered for it, expose weak premises without changing the subject, and explain what would count as evidence against their own preferred conclusion.

This makes debate a method of learning rather than merely competition.

## Why controversial propositions are useful

Questions such as simulation theory are useful because they force distinctions among:

* possible and actual;
* model and reality;
* correlation and causation;
* analogy and evidence;
* observation and interpretation;
* philosophical argument and empirical demonstration.

Future controversial units can use the same structure. The instructional objective is not controversy itself. The objective is **epistemic discipline**.

## The principle of charitable reconstruction

Before criticizing a position, a learner should be able to restate it in terms its defender would recognize. This discourages straw-man arguments.

A student earns stronger reasoning credit for answering a strong counterargument than for defeating an intentionally weak one.

## Evidence hierarchy

Students should learn to ask:

1. What was directly observed?
2. What measurement produced the observation?
3. What model explains it?
4. Are competing models possible?
5. What assumptions connect the evidence to the conclusion?
6. What prediction would distinguish the explanations?
7. What evidence could falsify the proposition?

## Science and philosophy

Physics can constrain philosophical claims, but philosophical questions often extend beyond what a single experiment can establish. The course should therefore label the type of claim being made.

A scientific measurement, mathematical model, metaphysical proposition, thought experiment, and analogy should never be silently treated as interchangeable.
