# Physics and Reality Topics

## Simulation theory

Simulation arguments ask whether an experienced physical world could be generated by an underlying information-processing system.

The course distinguishes philosophical simulation arguments from empirical physics. Features of physics may be compared with features of computation, but resemblance alone does not establish common cause.

## Limited rendering

Limited rendering is treated as a design hypothesis about simulations.

### Strong argument
A computational system has finite resources. Avoiding unnecessary computation can conserve resources. Therefore a sufficiently complex simulation might use demand-dependent representation.

### Important limitation
Even if this is an efficient simulation strategy, it does not follow that quantum measurement, finite information density, or any other feature of physics is caused by such a strategy.

That additional step requires independent evidence.

## Measurement and observation

Students should learn that “observer” in physics does not automatically mean a conscious human mind. Measurement generally involves physical interaction and information acquisition.

## Information

Information-theoretic descriptions of physics are powerful, but “physics can be described informationally” and “physics is literally software” are different propositions.

## Competing explanations

For each proposed simulation analogy, students should ask what conventional physical theory explains the same observation.

The course rewards the ability to state the strongest competing explanation.
